SLIDE 1 · TITLE  (~15 s)
============================================================================
"Good morning. My thesis is called Memory-Assisted LLM as a Judge via
Knowledge Graphs for Evaluating Agents. It asks two questions: when does
memory help an LLM judge, and how do we measure that in a way we can
trust. The work was supervised by Professor Bader Rasheed."


============================================================================
SLIDE 2 · LITERATURE REVIEW  (~35 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"Before I show what I built, I want to place this work in its context.
It sits between three lines of prior research."
(click)
"The first — LLM-as-a-Judge. Papers like MT-Bench, G-Eval, and Prometheus
showed that an LLM can grade another model's answers. The agreement with
humans is high. But these judges also have biases — for example, the
position of the answer, or its length. So we cannot fully trust them.
The judge itself needs to be checked."
(click)
"The second — memory for models. RAG, MemGPT, Reflexion, Generative
Agents. They all give a model a memory of past things. The idea is
simple: with memory, the model should do better. But none of these
papers ask what happens if the memory leaks into the test. They just
trust it."
(click)
"The third — evaluation safety. Recent work, like Balloccu and
PoisonedRAG, shows that scores get inflated when test data leaks into
training or memory. This is a real, known problem."
"So my work sits at the meeting point of these three. A judge, with
memory, evaluated under strict checks."


============================================================================
SLIDE 3 · MOTIVATION  (~25 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"Today, LLMs are graded by other LLMs."
(click) "We use them to rank chatbots, to score agents, even as reward
signals during training."
(click) "But a stateless judge has no memory. It cannot be consistent
across related questions."
(click) "So the idea was — give the judge a memory of its past
evaluations, and it should do better."

"My first build was the memory itself — a typed knowledge graph in Neo4j

(click) "Policy — the grading rule the judge follows."
(click) "Attempt — a past verdict that the judge gave."
(click) "Issue — a specific problem the judge found in an attempt."
(click) "Fix — a way to fix that problem."
(click) "Semantic — an abstract category that groups similar issues."
(click)
"And four relationships link them — SATISFIES, CAUSES, RESOLVES, and
ABSTRACTS_TO."

SLIDE 5 · END-TO-END EXAMPLE  (~60 s · 6 clicks)
============================================================================
"Before I show the example, one short note on how retrieval works in
MAJ. It is a three-stage pipeline. For every input, the judge runs
three queries on the graph, one after the other. Stage one looks for
past attempts that are similar. Stage two looks for issues that were
flagged in the past. Stage three looks for recurring semantic patterns.
Each stage uses cosine similarity, with its own threshold. The three
results are then combined into one block of context for the judge."

"Now — here is what that pipeline actually produces on one real
EvalsBench item."
(click) "The input is a task about pre-money valuation of a startup,
plus a candidate answer that talks about DCF, comparable companies,
and the VC method."
(click) "Stage one — past attempts that are similar. We get two that
passed, and one that failed."
(click) "Stage two — similar issues that the judge found before."
(click) "Stage three — recurring patterns. For this item, we get the
category 'incomplete methodology', which was seen in four past attempts."
(click) "All three stages are put together into one block of context."
(click) "GPT-4o then gets the task, the rubric, the retrieved context,
and the candidate answer. It gives back a structured verdict — pass or
fail, with a reason."

SLIDE 7 · BUILDS #2 + #3 · MCTS EXTENSIONS  (~35 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"Next, I added two extensions that use Monte Carlo Tree Search."
(click)
"Build two — MCTS-Retrieval. It does tree search over seven retrieval
actions on the graph. Four of them follow multiple hops, going from one
type of node to another."
(click)
"Build three — MCTS-Judge. It splits one evaluation into five to seven
small subtasks. Each rollout picks the next subtask using UCT, and the
final verdict comes from the best path."
(click)
"Together, this gave me six different evaluation modes. I could turn
memory on or off, and search on or off, and compare."


============================================================================
SLIDE 8 · MCTS-JUDGE WALKTHROUGH  (~40 s · 5 clicks)
============================================================================
"Here is how MCTS-Judge runs end to end."
(click) "Input — the same task and candidate answer."
(click) "Decompose — the LLM generates five to seven small subtasks,
like 'Does it cover DCF?' or 'Is the discount rate logic correct?'."
(click) "Search — four rollouts, depth five. At each step, the next
subtask is picked using UCT plus an LLM self-check."
(click) "Aggregate — we take a majority vote on the best path, and a
final synthesis over all subtasks."
(click) "Verdict — pass or fail, with reasoning."

SLIDE 10 · EARLY SIX-MODE BENCHMARK · SURPRISE  (~60 s · 7 clicks)
============================================================================
"Before I show this slide — let me set the context. The very first
benchmarks I ran were small, and they looked promising. On a unit-test
evaluation, accuracy went from 90 percent stateless to 100 percent with
memory. On EvalsBench with GPT-4o, 78 to 85. On Claude, 82 to 88.
Gains of 6 to 10 points. These were preliminary — not yet audited —
but they made me expect that scaling up the system would only help more."

"Then I added the two MCTS extensions and ran a fuller, six-mode
benchmark — and got a surprise."
(click) "Stateless — 60 percent."
(click) "MAJ with memory — 63."
(click) "MCTS-Judge alone — 56. Worse than stateless."
(click) "MCTS-Judge with memory — 60. No gain."
(click) "MCTS-Retrieval with the simple judge — 60."
(click) "Full MCTS — both layers together — 46."
(click)
"This was only thirty samples, and the run was not audited. In the
thesis, this is reported as a motivating observation, not as a verified
result. But the picture was clear — something was wrong. Either the
system was broken, or the measurement was."

SLIDE 11 · DIVIDER · PART 3 — CAN WE TRUST?  (~15 s)
============================================================================
"So I stopped, and went back to check the measurement. The first numbers
were too good. The next numbers were too strange. Both said the same
thing — maybe the benchmark itself is lying."


============================================================================
SLIDE 12 · TWO LEAKAGE CHANNELS  (~50 s · 2 clicks)
============================================================================
"I found two ways the test can leak."
(click)
"The first — paired item leakage. EvalsBench has paired items. Every
question appears twice — once with a passing answer, once with a failing
one. If the two halves end up on different sides of the split, then
while the judge is grading the failing one, it can see the passing one
in memory. It already knows the answer."
(click)
"The second — write-back leakage. If we update memory during the test,
then the verdict on an early item shows up in memory when we grade a
later, related one. Every early write affects every later read."


============================================================================
SLIDE 13 · BUILD #4 · LEAKAGE-FREE PROTOCOL  (~30 s · 2 clicks)
============================================================================
"To close both — I designed a new protocol."
(click) "Fix one — split the benchmark by question, not by row. So both
halves of a pair always stay on the same side."
(click) "Fix two — freeze the memory during the test. Memory is built
once, before the test, and after that it is read-only."
"But a claim is not a proof. How can I show that the freeze really
held?"


============================================================================
SLIDE 14 · THE TWO-LAYER AUDIT  (~40 s · 2 clicks)
============================================================================
"That is what the audit does. It has two layers."
(click)
"Layer one — after the run. I take a fingerprint of the graph using
SHA-256, before and after. The run is accepted only if both fingerprints
are the same."
(click)
"Layer two — during the run. I patch every write method on the graph.
If anything tries to write, the system raises an exception immediately."


============================================================================
SLIDE 15 · AUDIT WALKTHROUGH  (~40 s · 5 clicks)
============================================================================
"To make it concrete —"
(click) "Before — I take the snapshot, count nodes and edges, and hash
to SHA-256."
(click) "During — I block every write method."
(click) "Run eval — all eighty test items are judged. Memory should not
change."
(click) "After — I take a second snapshot, and compare."
(click) "Verdict — the run passes only if the fingerprints match AND no
write was attempted. If either fails, the run is rejected."


============================================================================
SLIDE 16 · SNAPSHOT DIFF · MONEY SLIDE  (~50 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"And the audit was not a formality. On the very first run, it caught a
real bug."
(click)
"On the left — what the audit looks like when the run is clean. 412
nodes, 412 nodes. Same number of edges. Fingerprints match."
(click)
"On the right — what actually happened. The graph grew. From 412 nodes
to 492. From 831 edges to 1003. The fingerprints did not match."
(click)
"It turned out the eval path was writing the judged item back into
memory. This is exactly the write-back leakage that the protocol is
there to stop. Without the audit, this bug would have silently inflated
every number in this thesis. I fixed it by adding a guard on the eval
path."

SLIDE 23 · POISONING ARTIFACT · BEFORE/AFTER  (~50 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"The audit also corrected a second false finding."
(click)

SLIDE 24 · BUILD #5 · RELIABILITY HARNESS  (~40 s · 3 clicks)
============================================================================
"Accuracy is only one number. So I built a reliability harness — three

(click) "Stochastic stability — judge the same item twice. MAJ agrees

modes are stable."
(click) "Label flip — I flip the response from pass to fail. The judge
should flip too. All modes do this above chance."
(click) "Defense filter — under 50 percent poisoned memory, does a
similarity filter help? Answer — no, it does not. An honest negative
result."

SLIDE 28 · THREE PIECES OF EVIDENCE  (~40 s · 4 clicks)
============================================================================
(click) "First — oracle labels do not beat self-written. If the judge
was really using the labels from memory, perfect labels should help.
They do not."
(click) "Second — a bare schema works the same as the full schema. A
memory with only two node types is as good as the full five-node graph."
(click) "My explanation — GPT-4o is already near the top of its ability
on this task. A strong judge has little room left for memory to add
value."
(click) "The real test would be a weaker judge model. That is future
work."

EXPECTED QUESTIONS — ONE-LINE PLAIN ANSWERS
============================================================================
Q1. "Why does the audit matter if the result is null?"
A.  "Because the audited null result corrects two earlier false findings.
    Without the audit, the field would believe the wrong thing."

Q2. "Why n=80? Can you draw conclusions from that?"
A.  "n=80 is large enough to detect the 6 to 10 point claim that the
    earlier protocol made. It cannot detect small effects of a few
    points, and I say so on the threats slide."

Q3. "Did you try a weaker judge model?"
A.  "Not in this thesis. The reasoning-ceiling idea says memory should
    help a weaker model. That is the first item in future work."

Q4. "Why is MCTS reported as exploratory?"
A.  "Because under the audited protocol it gives no significant accuracy
    gain, while latency is nine times higher. Reporting it as recommended
    would not be honest."

Q5. "What is the contribution that survives beyond this benchmark?"
A.  "The leakage-free protocol and the audit. They are independent of
    the model and the benchmark. Any researcher can apply them."

Q6. "Can an attacker still leak through the audit?"
A.  "In theory, yes, through a channel that I do not model — for example,
    timing side channels. I treat the audit as defence in depth against
    the two channels I do model, not as a universal guarantee."
