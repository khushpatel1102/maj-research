DEFENSE Q&A PREP — BRUTAL PANEL SIMULATION
Three professors. Three angles of attack.
Read every question. Memorize every one-line answer.
============================================================================

The three personas:

  PROF A — THE STATISTICIAN
    Cares about: power, significance, sample size, multiple comparisons,
    methodology rigour. Will ask: "could you actually detect anything?"

  PROF B — THE SYSTEMS / ENGINEERING PROFESSOR
    Cares about: scope, cost, correctness, reproducibility, what's novel.
    Will ask: "what did you build that anyone else couldn't?"

  PROF C — THE PHILOSOPHER / META-CRITIC
    Cares about: framing, claims, intellectual honesty, what this work
    actually means. Will ask: "do you really know what you think you know?"


============================================================================
PROF A · THE STATISTICIAN
============================================================================

Q1. "Your n is 80. With McNemar at n=80, you can barely detect a
12-point difference. Why did you not collect more data?"
A.  "Sample size was bounded by EvalsBench — 160 paired items, 80
    questions, half-half split. Increasing n means a larger benchmark,
    which is the first item of future work. n=80 is large enough to
    detect the 6-to-10 point claim the conventional protocol made, so
    it is large enough to refute that specific claim — which is the
    test the thesis runs."

Q2. "Wilson CI assumes Bernoulli trials. Your trials are not
independent — they are paired. Why is Wilson appropriate here?"
A.  "Wilson is used for each mode's own accuracy estimate, where the
    Bernoulli assumption applies item by item. For comparing two
    modes I use the paired McNemar test, which conditions on
    discordant pairs and does not assume independence between the
    two modes."

Q3. "You ran six modes. You're doing multiple comparisons. Did you
correct for that?"
A.  "I declare a difference significant only when McNemar p is below
    0.05 AND the paired bootstrap interval excludes zero. That joint
    rule is conservative. I did not apply Bonferroni explicitly,
    because every primary comparison fails to reject already — adding
    correction would only widen the gap."

Q4. "Your bootstrap resamples are paired. How many resamples? Did
you check stability?"
A.  "Ten thousand resamples. I checked stability by re-running with a
    different seed and the CI endpoints moved by less than 0.5
    percentage points. The intervals reported in the thesis are
    stable to that resolution."

Q5. "Your MDE is 12 to 15 points. So you cannot rule out a small
real effect. Is your null result a real null, or just an under-powered
study?"
A.  "It is a real null for the size of effect the prior literature
    claimed. The 6-to-10 point gain that the un-audited protocol
    reported is fully inside my detection range, and it disappears
    under the audited protocol. I cannot rule out a smaller genuine
    effect of a few points — and I say so on the threats-to-validity
    slide."

Q6. "You retried failed API calls. How many samples were excluded
because of persistent failures? What if those samples are systematically
different?"
A.  "Across the audited runs, the number of permanently failed samples
    is at most one or two per condition, recorded in the per-sample
    CSV. They are excluded from the denominator. If those one or two
    items were systematically different, the maximum impact on
    accuracy would be about 1.5 percentage points — well below the
    significance threshold."

Q7. "Why three seeds and not more? Three seeds is barely enough."
A.  "Three seeds is enough to show that the per-mode spread across
    seeds is wider than the gap between modes — which is the
    methodological point. More seeds would refine the estimate, not
    change the qualitative conclusion. It is also in future work."

Q8. "The paired bootstrap assumes exchangeability. Justify."
A.  "Exchangeability holds because the same test items are scored by
    both modes, and I resample item-pairs, not individual mode-scores.
    This is the standard paired bootstrap on the difference statistic."

Q9. "You report 70% accuracy for stateless. That's not great. Why
should we trust the judge at all?"
A.  "70 percent is a property of GPT-4o on this benchmark, not of my
    method. The thesis is not about absolute accuracy — it is about
    whether memory significantly changes that accuracy. And the
    reliability harness shows the judge is stable and discriminates
    correctly above chance."


============================================================================
PROF B · THE SYSTEMS / ENGINEERING PROFESSOR
============================================================================

Q10. "What is genuinely novel here? Memory-augmented systems exist.
LLM-as-a-judge exists. Audits exist. What's new?"
A.  "Three things. One — the combination: a typed-graph memory used by
    a judge, evaluated under a protocol that closes both leakage
    channels by construction. Two — the audit itself: SHA-256 plus
    runtime write-blocking, which to my knowledge is the first audit
    that proves the absence of leakage on every run, not just asserts
    it. Three — a corrective empirical finding that the field had not
    surfaced before."

Q11. "Your typed graph has five node kinds. Why these five and not
others? Was the ontology validated?"
A.  "These five emerged from the structure of evaluation itself — a
    rule (Policy), an outcome (Attempt), what went wrong (Issue),
    what fixes it (Fix), and a category (Semantic). I did not formally
    validate this ontology against a corpus of judges. The bare-schema
    ablation in chapter 5 suggests that for THIS judge, the extra
    structure is not adding value. A weaker judge might benefit more —
    that is future work."

Q12. "Why Neo4j? Why not a simpler vector database?"
A.  "Two reasons. One — typed relationships, which a flat vector store
    does not give. Two — multi-hop traversals, which MCTS-Retrieval
    uses. For pure cosine retrieval, a flat store would have been
    enough. For the typed graph, Neo4j is the natural fit."

Q13. "Your audit catches writes to the graph. What about reads with
side effects? What about a model that uses the retrieved content to
influence later behavior even without writing?"
A.  "Read-with-side-effect leakage is exactly what the question-level
    split closes. The frozen-memory audit closes write-back leakage.
    The two together close the two specific channels I identified.
    I do not claim to close all conceivable channels — for example,
    timing side channels — and I say so on the threats slide."

Q14. "You used GPT-4o through an API. That is not reproducible. How
would another researcher reproduce your results next year, after the
model is deprecated?"
A.  "I pin the model version in every API call. All per-sample inputs
    and outputs are stored in the open repository. If the model is
    deprecated, the per-sample CSVs remain — the analysis is
    deterministic from there. Re-running the model is not strictly
    needed to reproduce my statistical claims."

Q15. "Your MCTS-Judge runs 4 rollouts at depth 5. Why those numbers?
Did you sweep them?"
A.  "Yes. An earlier sweep to 4 rollouts and depth 5 gave no
    significant accuracy benefit over 2 rollouts at depth 3, at four
    times the cost. The current configuration is the smallest one
    that gives the system room to search without being prohibitively
    slow."

Q16. "MCTS adds 9x latency for no gain. So why include it in the
thesis at all?"
A.  "Because the thesis is not 'recommend MCTS' — it is 'evaluate
    memory-augmented judging including the exploratory directions'.
    The MCTS components are reported as exploratory, and the null
    result on them is itself useful. It tells the field that test-time
    search on a single judge model, on this kind of task, does not
    pay off."

Q17. "The reliability harness is three independent experiments. Why
those three and not, say, adversarial inputs or out-of-distribution
testing?"
A.  "Those three address the three most direct properties of a judge
    that accuracy alone hides: does the judge repeat itself (stability),
    does it actually respond to the content (label-flip), can it be
    defended after poisoning (filter). Adversarial inputs and OOD are
    additional axes, and they are reasonable extensions — but they
    were beyond the scope of this thesis."

Q18. "How long does the full reproduction take? If I clone the repo
right now, when do I see results?"
A.  "About two hours on a single machine with API access. The
    reproduce_all.py script runs every audited mode, every poison
    condition, the harness, and regenerates every figure and statistic
    in the thesis."

Q19. "You did not implement defenses that work. So your defense filter
finding is negative. Why is that useful?"
A.  "Because a negative result on a defense, reported honestly, prevents
    other researchers from claiming the same filter without testing
    it. The thesis says: this specific filter does not help. It does
    not say: no filter can ever help. That is a tight, useful claim."


============================================================================
PROF C · THE PHILOSOPHER / META-CRITIC
============================================================================

Q20. "You say the audit makes the truth visible. But the audit is YOUR
artifact. How do we know it is not itself biased — that it is not
designed to produce the result it produced?"
A.  "Two ways. One — the audit's outputs are mechanical: a SHA-256 hash
    and an exception. They are not interpreted by me; they pass or
    fail deterministically. Two — the audit was built BEFORE the
    audited results were generated, and it found a real bug on its
    first run, which I had not anticipated. If it were designed to
    confirm a hypothesis, it would not have failed on my own code."

Q21. "Your headline finding is a null. Null results are usually not
publishable. Why is yours worth defending?"
A.  "Because it is a corrective null, not an unconditional null. It
    overturns two specific claims — a 10-point memory gain and a
    poisoning collapse — that the conventional protocol would have
    reported. Both were artifacts of measurement. The thesis is the
    proof that the conventional way of measuring this class of system
    is broken."

Q22. "You frame the contribution as methodological. But methodology
without strong empirical findings is hard to evaluate. How do we know
your protocol is actually good if it only produced a null?"
A.  "It produced two non-null findings — that a 10-point gain was
    fake, and that a 21-point poisoning crash was fake. Those are
    empirical findings, in the form of corrections. Without my
    protocol, both of those false findings would still be standing.
    The methodology is validated by the false claims it removed."

Q23. "Could it be that memory simply needs a different design? You
tried one design. Why generalise to 'memory does not help'?"
A.  "I do not generalise to that. The thesis claim is: under THIS
    protocol, with THIS model, on THIS benchmark, no significant
    benefit was detected for the four memory configurations I tested
    plus their MCTS extensions. The conclusion is local. The reasoning-
    ceiling hypothesis predicts that a different judge model might
    show a different result — that is future work."

Q24. "Bader's review mentioned you should think about when memory
helps. Have you? When does memory help?"
A.  "My mechanistic analysis suggests memory helps most when the judge
    is operating below its reasoning ceiling — that is, when the judge
    needs external signal to make the verdict. For a strong judge like
    GPT-4o on rubric-graded QA, the judge already has the signal.
    For a weaker judge, or for a more open-ended task without a clear
    rubric, memory should add value. That is the predictive
    consequence of the reasoning-ceiling idea."

Q25. "If you had to redo this thesis from scratch, what would you do
differently?"
A.  "Three things. One — larger benchmark from the start, so the
    statistical analysis is sensitive to smaller effects. Two — run
    the audit from day one, instead of discovering the need for it
    halfway through. Three — include at least one weaker judge model
    in the comparison, so the reasoning-ceiling hypothesis can be
    tested directly within the thesis rather than left to future
    work."

Q26. "You spent a year on this. Was it worth it, given the null
result?"
A.  "Yes. The system, the protocol, the audit, the harness, the
    statistical pipeline — all of those exist now, in an open
    repository, ready to be applied to the next memory-augmented
    judge that someone builds. And two false findings that would have
    propagated through the field are removed. That is what a year
    of careful work should produce."

Q27. "Why should anyone care about an LLM judge with memory?"
A.  "Because LLMs are being used as automatic graders for other LLMs —
    in benchmarks, in agent evaluation, in reinforcement learning from
    AI feedback. If judges are inconsistent or biased, every downstream
    decision is contaminated. Memory is one of the most natural
    extensions of LLM judging that has been proposed, and it deserves
    the rigorous evaluation that this thesis provides."

Q28. "Your title mentions 'evaluating agents'. But you evaluate single
LLM responses, not agents. Is the title accurate?"
A.  "The thesis title reflects the broader research direction — the
    judge is intended to evaluate agent outputs. The benchmark used,
    EvalsBench, contains responses that an agent or a model would
    produce on a structured task. For thesis scope, I focus on the
    judging step, which is independent of whether the candidate is
    a single LLM call or a multi-step agent."


============================================================================
ANTICIPATED TRICK / TRAP QUESTIONS
============================================================================

T1. "Can you summarise your thesis in one sentence?"
A.  "I built a memory-augmented LLM judge with a typed knowledge graph,
    designed a leakage-free protocol and a cryptographic audit to
    evaluate it, and used the audit to show that the apparent benefits
    of memory and the apparent fragility under poisoning were both
    measurement artifacts."

T2. "What is the single most important slide in your presentation?"
A.  "The snapshot diff slide. It is the visual proof that the audit
    caught a real bug — without it, every accuracy number in this
    thesis would be wrong."

T3. "Did you write all this code yourself?"
A.  "Yes, with standard libraries — Neo4j, OpenAI SDK, NumPy, SciPy,
    Pydantic. The architecture and the implementation are my own. The
    full source is in the open repository."

T4. "Did you use ChatGPT to write the thesis?"
A.  "I used AI tools to refine wording and check grammar, the same way
    one might use a proofreader. The ideas, the system design, the
    code, the experiments, and the analysis are mine."

T5. "Your supervisor's role was?"
A.  "Professor Bader Rasheed guided the research direction, gave
    critical feedback on the framing, and especially on whether the
    null result was real or an artifact — which directly led to the
    audit. The implementation and the writing are mine."

T6. "Which result are you most proud of?"
A.  "The catch on the first audit run. The audit found a real write-
    back leakage bug in my own code before any result was published.
    That is the moment the thesis stopped being a system demo and
    became a methodological contribution."

T7. "Which result are you least proud of?"
A.  "Not testing a weaker judge model. The reasoning-ceiling hypothesis
    is the most interesting predictive consequence of the thesis, and
    I leave it for future work because of time and compute. It is the
    first thing I would do if I continued this research."

T8. "Why this topic and not something else?"
A.  "Because LLM evaluation is becoming infrastructure for the rest
    of AI, and infrastructure that we cannot trust is dangerous. The
    chance to work on the measurement layer — rather than yet another
    new model — was exactly the kind of foundational contribution I
    wanted to make."

T9. "What did you learn personally from this work, beyond the result?"
A.  "I learned that intellectual honesty in research is a discipline.
    When my first numbers looked great, the easy thing was to write
    them up. The hard thing — and the right thing — was to ask whether
    they were real. That is the practice this thesis tried to embody."

T10. "Are you confident in your defense today?"
A.  "I am. The work is solid, the results are honest, and the protocol
    will outlast this thesis."

============================================================================
THE THREE THINGS YOU MUST NEVER SAY
============================================================================

1. NEVER say "memory does not help." Say: "no significant benefit was
   detected at this scale, on this benchmark, with this judge."

2. NEVER say "my system failed." Say: "the audited protocol corrected
   two findings that the un-audited protocol would have reported."

3. NEVER apologize for the null result. The null IS the result. Own it.
